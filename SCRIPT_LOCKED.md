# 4 AI Skills to Learn Before 2027 (So You Don't Fall Behind)

Imagine an AI draft saying the website launches Friday. You are about to send it to your manager. Then you open Slack and find Tuesday in a newer message, with a condition about testing attached.

Now you are back in the messages, trying to figure out what else the summary missed. The update is written. Working out whether you can send it is taking longer.

This is the sort of challenge I would want to solve before spending a lot of time learning an AI tool. Before 2027 I would focus on prompting, research, automation and agents. Honestly I would leave agents for later once the first three things are working well.

Let's use the website project to practice these skills.

You have a shared Google Doc and a lot of Slack messages. You could copy them into ChatGPT and ask, "Summarize this." What you really need for your update is a clear and accurate account of what is happening especially when two people give different dates.

I would ask for something specific. "Find the launch date. Quote the message that supports it. Include the person who wrote it and the date. If people disagree, show me the disagreement. Do not make a decision yourself."

That is prompting. You are deciding what a helpful answer would look like before you ask for one.

You can also check if the model followed the instruction. Did it quote a message? Did it skip the person who said, "Tuesday only works if testing is done"? That condition could change your update.

Research is the second skill. A link at the end of an answer does not solve anything. You have to read what that link leads to.

For a tool comparison, I would be checking the pricing page. A twenty-dollar monthly price might mean paying for a whole year upfront. You also need to know whether the feature you came for is included. An AI answer saying "this one is cheaper" would leave me with a few questions.

Once you have a prompt that gives you a draft, there is another problem. Friday comes again. You are copying messages once more.

For the copying, you can use Make, Zapier or n8n. You pick the apps to connect and decide what should start the workflow. A form being submitted, for example. Then you set up what happens to that entry. For your first try, forget the whole project report. Try a form submission going into a spreadsheet. Submit a test entry. See if it appears in the right place.

You might find that you don't need AI for that at all.

An AI summary could be useful if the responses are long. I would keep the original answer beside it so there is something to compare it with. And before the next batch arrives, send a blank submission through your workflow. Send the same entry twice as well. See whether you end up with empty rows or duplicate records.

I would leave the full project report for later. You still have to arrange access to the relevant messages and documents, and that depends on what the apps allow. Get one connection working before adding the other. Keep the finished report as a draft until you have read it.

Agents come into the picture when you need help working out the next step.

Suppose the Slack thread just says, "Use the revised schedule." No link. That is awkward for a workflow built to read one particular file. An agent with access to your files could look for the schedule, open a likely match, and use its contents to decide what else to check. You would want to see which version it picked and what it did about that testing condition.

A fixed workflow could handle parts of this too. The useful difference is how much of the path you can plan ahead.

I would give the agent a narrow task. Find the conflicting dates. Bring back the evidence. Leave the deadline alone. Do not send the update.

You still review its work, including whether it missed a file it couldn't open. A confident answer won't tell you that on its own.

If your current automation already gets everything you need, I would keep it. An agent gives you more decisions to check, and sometimes that is extra work you don't need.

Before opening another tutorial, I would pull up a task from last week and look at the part that needed fixing. Write that requirement into the prompt. Then try it on a second example, because getting one good answer is not much of a test.

When that works, look at the copying you do every week. That is your automation candidate. You can come back to agents when you have a task that needs one.
